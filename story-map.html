Soils of the Siskiyous

A Landscape Born from Fire and Time

In southern Oregon, where the ancient Klamath Mountains meet the volcanic Cascades, lies a remarkable testament to geological complexity: the Cascade-Siskiyou National Monument.

This unique landscape tells a story written in soils—a story of ancient ocean floors thrust skyward, volcanic eruptions that shaped the land, and millions of years of weathering that created some of North America's most distinctive soil ecosystems.

CLORPT: The Recipe for Soil

Hans Jenny's fundamental equation of soil formation—CLORPT—describes how five state factors interact to create soils. In CSNM, this framework reveals how extraordinary geological and ecological diversity produces one of North America's most complex soil landscapes.

Climate

Temperature and moisture patterns that drive weathering and organic matter accumulation

Organisms

Living organisms that cycle nutrients and build soil organic matter

Mediterranean Climate Regime

CSNM experiences a Mediterranean climate with cool, wet winters (40-60 inches annually) and hot, dry summers. This seasonal pattern drives distinctive weathering processes, creating soils with concentrated nutrients in surface horizons and deep clay accumulation zones.

Biodiversity Hotspot

The monument hosts over 300 plant species including endemic serpentine specialists. Mixed conifer forests, oak woodlands, and grasslands each contribute unique organic matter inputs and root systems that shape soil structure and chemistry.

Complex Topography

Elevation ranges from 1,200 to 7,533 feet create distinct soil catenas. Steep slopes experience high erosion rates forming shallow Entisols, while valley bottoms accumulate colluvium developing deep Mollisols. Aspect controls moisture retention and vegetation patterns.

Geological Diversity

CSNM's soils develop from serpentinite (ultramafic), volcanic basalts and andesites, marine sediments, and granitic intrusions. Serpentine parent materials create unique soils high in magnesium and heavy metals but low in calcium, supporting rare endemic plant communities.

Soil Age Variations

Soil ages range from recent volcanic deposits (<10,000 years) to ancient stable surfaces (>1 million years). Older soils show deep weathering profiles with distinct clay-enriched B horizons, while younger soils retain parent material characteristics.

Soil Texture and Particle Classes

The physical properties of soils—their texture and particle size distribution— determine water retention, nutrient availability, and plant growth potential.

CSNM's diverse parent materials create soils ranging from coarse, rocky textures in steep terrain to fine, clay-rich soils in protected valleys. Each texture class supports different plant communities and ecological functions:

  • Fine textures: Clay-rich soils with high water retention
  • Coarse-loamy: Well-drained soils with balanced drainage
  • Loamy-skeletal: Rocky soils with excellent drainage
  • Medial: Volcanic soils with unique properties

Texture Determines Function

Different soil textures create unique growing environments, from drought-tolerant plants on coarse soils to moisture-loving species in fine-textured areas.

Elevation and Topographic Influence

From valley floors at 1,200 feet to Mt. Ashland's peak at 7,533 feet, elevation creates distinct soil zones across the monument's landscape.

Temperature, precipitation, and growing season length all change dramatically with elevation, creating a complex mosaic of soil development patterns. Higher elevations experience slower soil formation due to:

Lower Temperatures

Cooler temperatures at high elevations slow chemical weathering and organic matter decomposition

Shorter Growing Season

Limited frost-free periods reduce biological activity and soil development rates

Steep Slopes

Gravity and erosion move materials downslope, creating thin soils on ridges and deeper soils in valleys

Soil Chemical Properties

Understanding soil chemistry—from carbon storage to pH levels—reveals how these soils support plant life and store essential nutrients.

Scientists examine soil properties at different depths to understand how water, nutrients, and organic matter move through the profile. Two key properties tell important stories:

Organic Carbon Storage

CSNM's soils store significant amounts of carbon in organic matter, playing a crucial role in nutrient cycling and carbon sequestration. Darker areas show higher carbon content.

Soil Acidity

pH levels vary dramatically across the monument, from acidic forest soils (blue-green) to more neutral grassland soils (yellow-green), each supporting different plant communities.

Geological Foundation

The bedrock beneath CSNM tells a story of ancient oceans, volcanic eruptions, and mountain-building events that created the foundation for today's soils.

Understanding lithology—the physical and chemical characteristics of rocks— helps explain why different areas of the monument have such distinct soil properties and support different ecosystems:

Volcanic Origins

Volcanic rocks and ash deposits create nutrient-rich parent materials that weather into fertile soils

Serpentine Complex

Ancient oceanic crust and mantle rocks create unique, mineral-rich soils with specialized plant communities

Sedimentary Layers

Layered sedimentary rocks provide varied mineral compositions and drainage characteristics

Metamorphic Rocks

Heat and pressure-transformed rocks create complex mineral assemblages that influence soil chemistry
